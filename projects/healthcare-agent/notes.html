A hospital already knew how to spot sepsis. Its screening tool caught 78% of cases — and clinicians threw away 94% of its alerts. This is the product built to fix that, taken apart decision by decision: what it does, why each part exists, and which part to look at when it breaks.
REVERSE BLUEPRINT — CH.13, 30 AGENTS EVERY AI ENGINEER MUST BUILD1

1The problem was never detection

The tool already worked

qSOFAa bedside sepsis checklist ran for three years at 78% sensitivity. Detection was largely solved.

It fired on 22% of patients — roughly 43 alerts a shift.

Clinicians dismissed 94% of them.

So it stopped working

A tool ignored 94% of the time has an effective sensitivity near zero, whatever the paper says.

Staff called it “the boy who cried wolf.”

14 missed cases last year. 3 deaths. ~$280K each.

The actual job

Not a better sensor. An alert worth believing.

Every design choice downstream serves that, and most of them are about saying less, not more.

Target: 43 alerts/shift → under 12.
ELI10: the smoke alarm worked fine. It went off so often that everyone took the battery out. The job isn’t a better sensor — it’s an alarm people don’t want to silence.

3Six parts, and none of them knows what sepsis is

FIGURE 3 — A general engine plus one swappable rulebook

THE ENGINE

condition-agnostic — never mentions sepsis

1 Ingest

Any format in, one shape out. Places events on a timeline. Reports what is missing.

2 Scoreboard

Ranked candidates with likelihoods. Evidence multiplies in; the list renormalises to 100%.

3 Safety gate

Reads the scoreboard, asks one question. Its own owner, its own change log.

4 Explain

Turns the scoreboard into sentences, per audience. Fill-in-the-blank templates.

5 Audit

Append-only, hash-chained record of every scoring pass.

6 Deliver

Pushes the one-liner to triage and the page to the attending.

THE CONDITION PACK

the rulebook it reads at startup

Candidate list

What it is allowed to consider.

Starting odds

Where every scoreboard begins.

Evidence weights

How much each finding shifts each candidate.

Escalation threshold

The bar that pages a human.

ACTIVE: sepsis

Three more packs — heart attack, clot, stroke — sit complete in _archive. Never imported.

Enabling a fourth condition = move a folder, edit config. Zero engine changes.

ELI10: the six parts are a scoreboard, a scorer and a referee. The condition pack is the rulebook telling them which sport they are refereeing today. Swap the rulebook and they will happily referee something else without noticing.

Kept, not deleted

The three unused conditions stay as complete, valid packs. Nothing imports them; a startup check fails loudly if anything tries.

The original book code is never modified.

Expansion is governance

Each new condition needs its own odds and weights, derived by a clinician and signed. And it spends from a fixed alert budget.

Not an engineering cost. A sign-off cost.

It travels

Ranked hypotheses, calibrated confidence, conservative escalation, audit trail, human decides.

The same skeleton is a FinCrime investigation copilot.

The total is always exactly 1.0 — the code asserts it. That guarantee is a contract with every layer downstream.

Why not a yes/no

A binary tool has one knob, so sensitivity and alert volume trade 1:1 forever. You are stuck on a single line.

A ranked list is how clinicians already think. That is an adoption argument, not an accuracy one.

Why it must be live

Evidence arrives asynchronously and each piece updates the same object. No waiting for a complete record — waiting was the old bug.

Latency IS the metric: 4.2 hours → 1.1 hours.

The trap

The priorsthe starting odds, before any evidence arrives are five numbers in a config file. Change them and every future diagnosis changes — silently, no code edit, no retraining.

Nobody owns that file. See §12.

The output is an interrupt, not a document. Nobody logs in to check the sepsis dashboard — a dashboard nobody opens has the same effective sensitivity as an alert everybody dismisses.

5The safety gate — 0.15 is a price, not a setting

FIGURE 5 — What the number actually buys

A candidate crosses the scoreboard

Sepsis reaches some confidence.

→

Above 0.15?

e.g. urosepsis at 0.61

→

PAGE A HUMAN

Immediately. No discretion.

→

Above 0.15, but only just?

pneumonia-source at 0.21

→

STILL PAGES

A 21% chance of sepsis demands a human look. A 0.5 bar would have dropped it silently.

→

Below 0.15, non-critical?

low confidence, ambiguous findings

→

FLAG FOR REVIEW

Presents no diagnosis. Low confidence produces more human involvement, never less.

There is no path through this system where the agent becomes more autonomous as it becomes less sure.

What 0.15 means

“We will accept about six unnecessary reviews to avoid one missed sepsis.”

An explicit exchange rate between a clinician’s time and a patient’s life.

Somebody signs that. No engineer can. That is the job.

Where it came from

Cost asymmetrya miss costs far more than a false alarm: a missed case runs roughly 10× a false alarm. The break-even lands in 0.12–0.18.

0.15 is the midpoint, deliberately rounded conservative.

Another hospital, different ICU costs, lands elsewhere in that range.
Also: §13 who may change it · §4 what it reads

Who can move it

Nobody, at runtime. It lives in version control. There is no admin screen.

Changing it needs a written justification, a signature and a logged deployment.

A dial that exists will get turned during a bad week.
The gate is a separate component from the scorer on purpose. Fuse them and tuning accuracy silently retunes safety — and nobody notices which change did it.
ELI10: the 15% isn’t a technical setting, it’s a promise about how many false alarms you’re willing to put up with so that nobody dies. Someone has to put their name to that promise, and it shouldn’t be whoever last touched the code.

THE MISSING PIECE

two controls, one undocumented

Control 1 — who gets a scoreboard

Never specified anywhere. Does essentially all the volume management.

Control 2 — the 0.15 bar

Does almost none of it. The odds already clear it.

DECISION

Score everyone, starting from true population odds — low single digits, not 25%. No binary cutoff anywhere.

A pre-screen on vitals would have been qSOFA again, one layer earlier — and subtle cases are exactly what binary checklists miss.

ELI10: somebody told you the alarm was set sensibly. True — but nobody wrote down which rooms get an alarm fitted in the first place, and that decision is doing all the work.
Found by the reader’s question, not by the book. This is the strongest thing in the whole study — and it is a specification gap, which is the kind a product manager catches and an engineer does not.

The missing piece has a name worth keeping: the entry gatewhatever decides which patients get scored at all. Every alerting product has one. Most never write it down, because it usually lives in a filter someone added early and nobody revisited.

Read this before the questions start, not after. Fifteen decisions, grouped by document. Each one is a genuine fork — both branches were defensible, and the choice changed what got built.

  • D05 Whose interest sets the bar. Catch rate, or alert trust?
  • D06 Minimum launch. Serve the main user roughly, or the senior user fully?

Doc 2 Technical — 5 calls

  • D08 Any AI model at all? None, local, or cloud?
  • D09 Go-live bar. How long silent, and what must it prove?

  • D11 When it breaks. Show it, hide it, or refuse to answer?
  • D12 Audit write fails. Page anyway, or stay silent?

Doc 3 Security & Access — 4 calls

  • D13 Who sees a scoreboard. Care team only, everyone on shift, or care team plus a logged override?
  • D14 Who can move the 15%. Nobody, an admin screen, or auto-tuned to hold volume?
  • D15 Reflex dismissal. Require a reason, watch patterns only, or trust and do nothing?
  • Repeat alerts. Silent until confidence rises, one per time window, or one per visit ever?
The abuse risk here is not intrusion. It is a tired clinician clicking “dismiss”.

Docs 4–5 Frontend & Tickets — 4 calls

  • Local view. Something to look at, terminal only, or a full dashboard?
  • The look. Reference screenshots, clinical-dark, or clean-light? (Still a risk — no screenshots given.)
  • The silent state. Nothing on screen, a quiet marker, or always show the score?
  • Pager text, ~15 words. Add the action, add the evidence, or name the condition only?
  • What to build first. Vertical slice, dependency order, or tests first?

WHERE TO LOOK

one part, every time

The evidence feed

Nothing is arriving. Scoreboard is frozen at its last state.

One source, in ingest

Labs are down. Scoring continues on what did arrive.

A component threw

Fallback answer returned. The log names the component.

The audit store

Can’t write. The alert still fires — patient safety outranks record-keeping.

The starting odds

Case mix has drifted. Tracked as a live metric with an alarm.

Clock skew between systems

Weakens the trajectory signal, which is where the sepsis evidence lives.

Never show a raw error dump. Never let a degraded answer look like a normal one.

Why silence needs a marker

For ~172 of 180 patients the agent has nothing to say. If it renders nothing, then silence means both “this patient is fine” and “the system is broken”.

A quiet “monitored, last checked 14:32” tells the two apart.

The one deliberate exception

If the tamper-evident log can’t be written, the alert still goes out, and a separate alarm tells IT the logbook is broken.

An unrecorded clinical decision is a compliance breach — so this needs written sign-off, not a code comment.

SHADOW MODE

One full quarter

The headline number

Useful alerts per shift. Before: 43 × 6% acted ≈ 2.6. After: 8 × 82% acted ≈ 6.6.

5× fewer alerts, 2.5× more action. Not a trade — the old volume was destroying its own value.

One column decides

Every alert row records acted / dismissed / no response. The headline metric is computed from that column alone.

Don’t capture it and the product cannot prove it worked.

12What is still unresolved

O1 — Nobody owns the starting odds

Scoring everyone needs true population odds from this hospital’s own history. That is clinical analysis, not engineering, and no one is assigned to it.

If they come from a population unlike your patients, you have a bias engine with a mathematical alibi — trusted more than a black box, because it shows its working.

The biggest unaddressed risk across all five documents.

O2 — The gate reads words, not codes

The safety check matches condition names as free text. A case recorded as “septicaemia” or “septic shock” would not escalate.

Everything else in the system insists on coded medical vocabularies. The single most safety-critical line does not.

Nothing crashes. It just quietly fails to page anyone.

O4 — Is 15% per hospital?

The cost-asymmetry gives a 0.12–0.18 range. A different hospital lands elsewhere in it.

If configurable per customer: pricing, liability and support consequences nobody has discussed.

O5 — No memory between visits

State is keyed on the visit, not the person. A patient returning three days later starts from scratch.

Correct for privacy. Possibly a lost clinical signal.

O7 — Nobody costed it

The book states savings (~$3.8M/yr) and never states build or licence cost.

The uncosted item is attending attention — the only budget that can bankrupt this product.

Audit database path

No model ID

Live patient state

One row per visit: the current scoreboard, when it last updated, how complete the data is, which sources have reported.

The audit log

One row per scoring pass: what evidence existed, the scoreboard either side of the update, which rulebook version was in force, the bar at that moment, whether it escalated, whether it was degraded.

Chained so a deleted row is detectable. Never updated by anyone, including an administrator.

Alerts

One row per page sent, plus acted / dismissed / no response and the one-tap reason.

The only fields a human may ever write.
